10 UX Laws Every Product Team Should Know

UX laws are named psychological principles that describe how people actually perceive and interact with interfaces. They're not arbitrary rules — they're observations about human cognition that hold true across products. Knowing them gives product teams a shared vocabulary and a set of evidence-based defaults, so debates about design decisions can reference established principles rather than personal opinion. Here are the ten that come up most often in real product work, each with practical application.

1. Hick's Law

The principle: The time it takes to make a decision increases with the number and complexity of choices available.

Practical application: Every additional option on a screen slows users down and increases the chance they'll abandon the decision entirely. This is why simplifying navigation, reducing form fields, and limiting choices at decision points (like pricing pages — see our pricing page article) reliably improves conversion. When in doubt, reduce choices.

2. Fitts's Law

The principle: The time to reach a target depends on the distance to it and its size — bigger, closer targets are faster to hit.

Practical application: Make important, frequently-used buttons larger and place them where users' attention (and, on mobile, their thumbs) naturally rests. Tiny buttons crammed into corners violate Fitts's Law and frustrate users. This is especially critical for mobile (see our thumb-zone considerations in the mobile design trends article).

3. Jakob's Law

The principle: Users spend most of their time on other products, so they expect your product to work the way those other products do.

Practical application: Don't reinvent established patterns without a compelling reason. Users bring expectations from every other product they use — a shopping cart icon, a hamburger menu, a settings gear. Novelty for its own sake creates friction; convention reduces the learning curve. Innovate on your core value, not on where the "save" button lives.

4. Miller's Law

The principle: The average person can hold about 7 (plus or minus 2) items in working memory.

Practical application: Don't overload users with too much information at once. Chunk content into digestible groups, use progressive disclosure (show more only when needed), and avoid asking users to remember information across steps. This principle underlies good information architecture and multi-step flow design.

5. The Peak-End Rule

The principle: People judge an experience largely based on how they felt at its most intense point (the peak) and at its end, rather than the average of every moment.

Practical application: Invest disproportionately in the emotional high points and the ending of key experiences. A great onboarding "aha moment" (the peak) and a satisfying task completion (the end) shape how users remember the whole product — even if some middle steps were mediocre. See our onboarding UX article for how this applies to activation.

6. Aesthetic-Usability Effect

The principle: Users perceive aesthetically pleasing designs as more usable — and are more tolerant of minor usability issues in beautiful products.

Practical application: Visual polish isn't superficial — it directly affects perceived usability and user trust. This is part of why design quality affects business outcomes (see our business value of design article). But it cuts both ways: aesthetics can mask real usability problems in testing, so don't let a beautiful prototype substitute for actual usability validation.

7. The Von Restorff Effect (Isolation Effect)

The principle: When multiple similar items are present, the one that differs most is the most likely to be remembered and noticed.

Practical application: Make the most important element visually distinct. This is why a primary call-to-action should stand out from secondary actions, and why the "recommended" pricing tier gets a badge or color treatment. But use it sparingly — if everything is emphasized, nothing is.

8. The Doherty Threshold

The principle: Productivity soars when a system responds to user actions within 400 milliseconds — fast enough that the interaction feels immediate.

Practical application: Speed is a UX feature, not just an engineering concern. Interfaces that respond within ~400ms keep users engaged and productive; slower responses break flow and increase abandonment. When true speed isn't possible, use feedback (loading states, optimistic UI, skeleton screens) to maintain the perception of responsiveness.

9. Tesler's Law (Conservation of Complexity)

The principle: Every system has an inherent amount of complexity that cannot be removed — it can only be shifted between the user and the design/system.

Practical application: Complexity doesn't disappear; someone has to deal with it. Good design absorbs complexity into the system so the user doesn't have to. When a task feels simple to the user, it's usually because the design took on the complexity behind the scenes. Ask: are we making this simple for us at the cost of the user, or absorbing the complexity so the user's experience is simple?

10. The Zeigarnik Effect

The principle: People remember incomplete or interrupted tasks better than completed ones.

Practical application: Progress indicators, completion bars, and "you're 60% done" prompts leverage this — the sense of an incomplete task motivates users to finish. This is why progress bars in onboarding and setup flows measurably improve completion rates. It also explains why abandoned-cart reminders work.

How to Actually Use These Laws

UX laws are defaults and diagnostic tools, not rigid rules. Their practical value:

  • As a shared vocabulary — "this violates Hick's Law" is a faster, less personal way to flag a problem than "I feel like there are too many options"
  • As design defaults — when you're unsure, these principles point toward the choice more likely to work
  • As diagnostic tools — when something feels off in a design, checking it against these laws often reveals why
  • Not as absolute rules — every law has exceptions, and real design requires judgment about when a principle applies and when a specific context overrides it

The teams that use these laws well treat them as a starting point for better decisions and clearer discussion — not as a checklist to blindly follow.

FAQ

Are UX laws scientifically proven? Most are grounded in well-established psychology and human-factors research (Fitts's Law and Hick's Law, for example, come from experimental psychology). They describe reliable patterns in human cognition and behavior — but they're principles with context-dependent exceptions, not absolute physical laws.

Do I need to memorize all these laws to design well? No — but knowing them gives your team a shared vocabulary and evidence-based defaults that make design discussions faster and less subjective. Even understanding the handful most relevant to your product (Hick's, Fitts's, Jakob's, and Peak-End for most products) meaningfully improves decision quality.

Which UX law has the biggest impact on conversion? Hick's Law (reducing choices) and the Doherty Threshold (speed) both have direct, measurable conversion impact for most products. Reducing decision complexity and improving perceived speed are two of the most reliable conversion levers UX offers.

Can following UX laws ever hurt a product? Yes — applied rigidly without judgment, they can. Jakob's Law taken too far discourages any innovation; the Aesthetic-Usability Effect can lead teams to over-value polish and skip real usability testing. The laws are tools for better judgment, not substitutes for it.
