Jobs-to-be-Done: How to Design Features Users Actually Want

Jobs-to-be-Done (JTBD) is one of the most useful reframes in product thinking, captured in a deceptively simple idea: people don't buy products, they "hire" them to get a job done. Understanding the job — the underlying progress a user is trying to make — is what separates products that solve real problems from products that pile up features nobody asked for. This guide covers what JTBD actually means and how to apply it to design features users genuinely want.

The Core Idea

The famous illustration of JTBD is the milkshake story: a fast-food chain trying to sell more milkshakes studied why people bought them. It turned out many were bought by commuters who "hired" the milkshake for a specific job — something to make a boring morning commute more interesting, that was filling enough to last until lunch and could be consumed one-handed while driving. The competitors weren't other milkshakes; they were bananas, bagels, and boredom.

The lesson: the milkshake's real value had nothing to do with the attributes the company was optimizing (flavor variety, being a "treat"). It had everything to do with the job the commuter needed done. Understand the job, and you know what to build.

Why JTBD Matters for Design

Most feature bloat comes from designing around what users say they want (specific features) rather than the underlying job they're trying to accomplish. Users are excellent at describing their problems and terrible at prescribing solutions — they'll request a specific feature that addresses a symptom, when understanding their actual job would reveal a better solution.

JTBD keeps design focused on the job, not the requested feature. This connects directly to the research thinking in our user research guide (asking about past behavior, not hypothetical preferences) and to the outcome-over-output thinking in our roadmap prioritization guide. It's a lens for building the right thing, not just building things right.

The Anatomy of a Job

A well-articulated job has three parts:

  • The situation — the context and circumstances that trigger the need ("when I'm commuting in the morning...")
  • The motivation — what the user is trying to accomplish ("...I want something to make the drive less boring and keep me full...")
  • The expected outcome — the result that would make them consider the job done well ("...so I arrive at work satisfied and not hungry.")

A common format: "When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [expected outcome]." This structure forces you past the surface-level feature request to the underlying job.

Functional, Emotional, and Social Jobs

Jobs aren't only functional. Most decisions involve three dimensions:

  • Functional jobs — the practical task ("track my team's project progress")
  • Emotional jobs — how the user wants to feel ("feel in control and on top of things")
  • Social jobs — how the user wants to be perceived ("look organized and competent to my boss")

Products that address only the functional job miss much of what drives adoption and loyalty. A project management tool that tracks tasks perfectly but makes users feel overwhelmed has nailed the functional job and failed the emotional one — and users will leave for a competitor that makes them feel in control, even with fewer features.

How to Uncover the Jobs Your Users Hire You For

1. Interview Around Real Decisions

Talk to users about actual past decisions — when they started using your product (or a competitor's), what was happening in their life or work that triggered it, and what they were really trying to accomplish. This is behavioral, not hypothetical — the more reliable form of research (see our user research guide).

2. Look for the "Hiring" and "Firing" Moments

When did a user "hire" your product, and when do they "fire" it (churn)? These moments reveal the job most clearly — what changed such that the product was newly needed, or no longer got the job done.

3. Ask About Alternatives and Workarounds

What did users do before your product? What do they do when your product isn't available? The alternatives and workarounds reveal the job — because they're other ways of getting the same job done.

4. Separate the Job From the Solution

When a user requests a feature, ask what they'd accomplish with it. The requested feature is a proposed solution; the answer reveals the job. Design for the job, and you may find a better solution than the one they requested.

Applying JTBD to Design Decisions

Once you understand the jobs your users hire your product for:

  • Prioritize features by how well they serve important jobs — not by how many users request them
  • Evaluate new feature ideas against real jobs — does this serve a job users actually have, or a job we imagine they have?
  • Design flows around completing jobs — organize the product around getting jobs done, which often reveals better information architecture (see our IA guide)
  • Position and message around the job — marketing that speaks to the job resonates more than marketing that lists features

Common JTBD Mistakes

  • Defining jobs too narrowly (as features) — "hire our export button" isn't a job; "share my results with my team so they can act on them" is
  • Defining jobs too broadly (as generic goals) — "be productive" is too vague to design against
  • Ignoring emotional and social jobs — designing only for the functional job and missing what actually drives loyalty
  • Assuming the job instead of researching it — JTBD without real user research is just a fancier way of guessing
  • Treating JTBD as a one-time exercise — jobs evolve as users, markets, and contexts change

FAQ

Is Jobs-to-be-Done a research method or a design framework? Both — JTBD is a lens for understanding what users are really trying to accomplish (which requires research to uncover) and a framework for making design and prioritization decisions around those jobs. It connects research to design decisions, which is much of its value.

How is JTBD different from personas? Personas describe who the user is (demographics, characteristics); JTBD describes what the user is trying to accomplish and why. JTBD advocates argue the job is more stable and actionable than the persona — the same person "hires" products for different jobs in different situations, and it's the job, not their demographic profile, that determines what they need.

Can I use JTBD without extensive research? You need at least some real user input — JTBD applied to assumptions is just guessing with better vocabulary. But the research can be lean (a handful of behavioral interviews about real past decisions), following the budget-friendly methods in our user research guide. The key is grounding jobs in real user behavior, not internal assumption.

What's the biggest benefit of thinking in terms of jobs? It keeps you designing for what users are actually trying to accomplish rather than for the specific features they request. Users describe symptoms and propose solutions; understanding their underlying job often reveals a better solution than the one requested — and prevents the feature bloat that comes from building every requested feature.
