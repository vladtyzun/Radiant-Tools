How to Evaluate a Dribbble Portfolio for Hiring

Before looking at any specific designer, apply these four filters to any profile you're considering:

1. Process visibility, not just polished shots. Look for designers who show problem framing, iterations, and reasoning - not just final polished screens. A profile of exclusively beautiful final shots with zero visible process is a red flag for hiring purposes, even if the work looks impressive.

2. Real product context over concept work. Distinguish between shots labeled as "concept" or "personal project" versus shots tied to a named, shipped product. Concept work shows craft; shipped work shows the designer can navigate real constraints (stakeholders, technical limitations, business goals).

3. Range appropriate to your need. A designer with 200 shots of beautifully polished mobile app screens may not be the right fit if you need. Someone who can think through complex B2B dashboard information architecture - match the portfolio's actual demonstrated range to your specific problem, not just overall visual quality.

4. Communication signals in write-ups. Shot descriptions and case study write-ups reveal how a designer thinks and communicates — look for designers who can articulate why a decision was made, not just what it looks like.

Red Flags to Watch For

  • Shots with suspiciously uniform, templated layouts across many different "clients" - often signals template-based mockup generation rather than genuine custom design work — No visible client names or NDA acknowledgment for supposedly real client work - legitimate. Professional work usually has at least some attributable context, even if specific details are limited — Extremely high shot volume with very low individual engagement - can signal follower/engagement purchasing rather than organic portfolio quality, though this signal alone isn't conclusive — All shots in one extremely narrow, trendy visual style regardless of the underlying product/industry - may signal a designer applying one aesthetic template regardless of actual product context. Rather than adapting to genuine problem constraints.
